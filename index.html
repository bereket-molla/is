About

I was born and raised in Ethiopia 🇪🇹, and those were some of the best years of my life.

My first brush with computer science came in 2016 with a humble Visual Basic class. Back then, programming felt like magic spells on a screen. Over time I've come to see it more as a mix of math, more math (and some magic spells :) ).

These days I work at Amazon on the Promise Optimization team, the folks behind that delivery date we see when we order something. Before that, I interned twice under the Fulfillment Optimization Org, while studying computer science at Columbia University, mostly diving into systems programming with some machine learning on the side.

Lately, I've been learning about the mathematical foundations of robotic grasping through Why Linearity Shows Up in Grasps—an attempt to understanding why linear dimensionality reduction works so well for controlling complex robotic hands.

Rolang — Reactive Programming Language for Robots
Rolang is a small OCaml-like programming language I wrote with a friend during our senior year at Columbia after reading a paper on reactive functional languages. The aim was narrow and practical: a language with syntax that allows you to write invariants and a runtime that ensures those invariants hold in an event-driven manner as the program executes.

Why Linearity Shows Up in Grasps exploration
Professor Matei Ciocarlie briefly talked about his work on EigenGrasps my senior year in college. It is a technique inspired from the idea that a hand with many joints can be controlled with just a few "dials." Think about reaching for a mug. You don't explicitly think about setting each individual joint angle on your fingers. You rather "open your hands" and the joints move together in a certain pattern. That single pattern is a synergy.
